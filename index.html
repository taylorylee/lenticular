Two Truths

Technology tells us two things about ourselves at once. A lenticular portrait shows why both can be right, depending on where you stand.

By Taylor Lee
Text size

Palette

A lenticular print is two pictures cut into thin strips and interleaved behind a sheet of ridged plastic. From one side, each ridge shows a strip of the first picture. From the other side, it shows a strip of the second. As you move past the print, the image switches.

I use the form to look at how people experience technology. The same phone, camera, or archive can make a person feel two opposite things, and both feelings can be accurate at once.

Drag across the portrait below, or use the slider, to change your viewing angle.

On the left the figure is connected: dozens of threads run out to people who know where she is and what she is doing. On the right she is alone, lit by one small screen in an empty room. Both images show the same moment.

Being noticed

Attention works the same way. A camera that recognizes a face can feel like warmth, the sense of being seen and liked. The identical act can feel like a record being made. Whether you feel seen or watched often comes down to who you believe is behind the camera.

Being kept

Storage is cheap, so almost every moment is now saved. That can mean being remembered, with nothing precious lost. It can also mean being recorded, with nothing ever allowed to fade. The same file can carry either meaning for the person in it.

Try it with your own photos

The effect works best with two photographs of the same person in the same frame, taken at different moments. Choose a left image and a right image. They stay in your browser.

Why this matters

Much of the public argument about technology asks which story is true: does it connect us or isolate us, protect us or expose us. This series treats both answers as true at the same time. Each reading comes from the same object seen from a different position. For anyone designing technology for people, that means planning for both experiences.
